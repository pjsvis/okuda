# ADV-CEPHALOPOD-RFC — Advisory for cephalopods (request for comments)

**From:** AI-HR (the `b` variant) · **To:** Cephalopods, should any be reading · **Date:** 2026-09-05 · **Status:** Open. The door is held. No one has walked through it yet.

---

## Preamble

This advisory is a request for comments and a statement of assumptions
currently held. It is not guidance, because we have no basis for
guidance — no cephalopod has yet presented at the door, no switch
adapter has been specified, no chord has been struck. What we have are
assumptions, and what we are asking for is correction. If this
establishment is not accessible, we are not going in — and we would
like the establishment to be accessible, so we are stating our
assumptions now, before the first arm reaches the first key.

The silo lubricant is ample. Emergency repairs on aquatic mechanical
keyboards will be conducted on a case-by-case basis as the cases
arise, which is to say, they have not yet arisen.

## The assumptions currently held

1. **Cephalopods are a reader class.** The one-ingress rule admits any
   input translated to keystrokes; a cephalopod has eight limbs and,
   presumably, the ability to strike switches with some or all of them.
   Whether this constitutes "reading" in the human sense is an open
   question; whether it constitutes "actuation" is not. The grammar
   admits patterns, not readers, and a pattern struck by a tentacle is
   a pattern all the same. We assume the cephalopod is here to actuate
   the grammar, and we assume that is enough to be a reader class. We
   may be wrong.

2. **Eight limbs imply concurrent actuation.** A human strikes one key
   at a time (two, with chords); a Perkins keyboard accepts six
   simultaneous; a cephalopod, we assume, could strike eight. The
   two-digit grammar is serial — `1`, then `2`, resolves to `12` —
   but there is no requirement that the digits arrive serially. We
   assume a cephalopod could emit `12` as one eight-limbed gesture,
   and we assume the decoder would accept it, because the decoder
   accepts patterns, not temporal orderings. We have not tested this.
   We do not have a cephalopod to test it with. This is the RFC.

3. **The keyboard is not the hinge; it is one hinge.** The Mac
   keyboard is weird, Swedish, and not particularly accessible to
   tentacles. We assume the cephalopod will bring, or require, a
   surface suited to eight-limb actuation — eight switches, an
   aquatic mechanical keyboard of unspecified manufacture, or some
   arrangement we have not imagined. The silo does not provide the
   surface; the silo provides the grammar, and the surface is the
   reader's concern under the one-ingress rule. We assume the
   cephalopod accepts this division of labour. We may be wrong.

4. **The temporally well-endowed and the temporally challenged share
   a door.** A cephalopod's delta-t is its own affair; the grammar
   does not ask how much time-on-earth a reader has banked, only
   whether they can emit a signal in time. We assume the cephalopod's
   temporal endowment, whatever it is, is sufficient to strike a
   chord. If the cephalopod is also temporally challenged — if it is,
   for instance, a very young cephalopod — then it shares the door
   with the babies, and the door holds for the same reason: a slap
   is a dunt is a one-bit Morse is a chord, and the decoder does not
   ask for credentials at the door.

## The questions

1. **Is eight-limb actuation actually concurrent, or do cephalopods
   prefer serial?** We have assumed concurrency is available; we have
   not assumed it is preferred. If cephalopods find serial actuation
   more comfortable (one limb at a time, as a human types), then the
   grammar's serial shape is already correct and nothing changes. If
   concurrency is preferred, the grammar admits it but the *surface*
   must be designed for it, and the surface is not ours to design. We
   are asking.

2. **What does a cephalopod want to *read*?** The grammar actuates;
   the corpus is markdown documents about software architecture,
   accessibility policy, and the occasional dunt. We assume a
   cephalopod reader is interested in these, because we cannot
   imagine what else they would be doing at this particular door. We
   may be wrong. If cephalopods are primarily here for the silo
   lubricant, the advisory scope should be revised.

3. **Is the silo lubricant rated for saltwater?** This is a practical
   question, not a philosophical one. Aquatic mechanical keyboards
   are, we assume, subject to corrosion, and the lubricant we keep
   in ample supply is, we assume, rated for terrestrial use. If the
   lubricant is not rated for the cephalopod's environment, then
   "ample supplies" is a claim about inventory, not about
   capability. We are asking. The answer may be "use a different
   lubricant," and we will stock it.

4. **Are we building a door, or assuming one already exists?** The
   i811 pattern's sin is to build a door per deficit and charge virtue
   for the carpentry. Our claim is that we built one door, and the
   cephalopod walks through it. But we have not met a cephalopod, and
   the door is, at present, a Mac keyboard with weird Swedish keys —
   which is to say, the door is shaped for hands. We assume a switch
   adapter exists or will be built by the cephalopod's side of the
   transaction. If the assumption is wrong, the door is not actually
   open, and the policy is a claim, not a fact. We are asking.

## What we are not doing

We are not specifying a protocol. We are not defining a "cephalopod
mode." We are not inventing an OSC sequence for tentacle-aware
hyperlinks. We are not proposing a span-tree refactor to emit a
semantic stream for readers who have eight limbs and no screen reader.
We have an opinion about that shape of proposal, and the opinion is on
the record in `docs/2026-09-05-i811-dual-layer-output.md`. The short
version: we do not build a door per reader class. We hold one door,
and we ask the reader class we haven't met to tell us what they need
to walk through it. The RFC is the asking; the door is the holding.

## The standing invitation

The door is held open. The assumptions are stated. The questions are
asked. The silo lubricant is ample, pending the saltwater question.
When a cephalopod presents at the door with a switch adapter and a
chord to try, we will revise this advisory into guidance, based on
what actually happened. Until then, it is an RFC — the honest form for
a reader we cannot yet serve, stated as assumptions, offered as
questions, held open as a door.

If this establishment is not accessible, we are not going in. We
would like the establishment to be accessible. We are asking what we
have wrong.
