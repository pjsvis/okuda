# feck protocols

## the feckin' problem

- we have a device called the Shite Filter but feel that we require one or more tasteful alternatives
- the Brave in-browser AI suggested the following:

| Register | Term | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Industry standard** | Slop filter | Dismissive but accepted; the default now |
| **Slightly literary** | Dross filter | Archaic, evocative; "dross" = waste residue from smelting |
| **Technical / neutral** | Signal-to-noise filter | Clinical; removes the contempt |
| **British formal** | Refuse filter | Cold, institutional; "refuse" as noun = waste |
| **Industrial** | Waste filter | Flat, functional, zero personality |
| **Concise / punchy** | Cull | One word; implies active selection, not passive filtering |
| **Irish-adjacent** | Manky filter | Keeps the dialectal flavour without the swear |

- the _Slop Filter_ sounds good for consumer applications
- industrial or enterprise applications can use the _Dross Filter_ variant
- the _Shite Filter_ can remain for development work as retains its precision over time

## introdcution

The Irish have a whole ecosystem of milder words that fill the same slots. The closest direct substitutes for *shite* (meaning "something bad/broken/annoying") are:

| Word | Meaning | Roughly equivalent to |
|---|---|---|
| **Feck** | Exclamation of frustration | "Damn" / "Darn" |
| **Eejit** | Lovable idiot | "Dope" / "Muppet" |
| **Gobshite** | Nonsense-talker | "Blatherer" (contains *shite* but is considered milder and more humorous) |
| **Bollocks** | Exclamation of annoyance | "Ah bollocks!" ≈ "Ah shite!" (slightly stronger) |
| **Gammy** | Broken / useless | "Knackered" |
| **Brock** | Bad / lousy | "Crap" |
| **Clem** | Bad / off | "Dodgy" |
| **Manky** | Dirty / gross | "Filthy" |
| **Mouldy** | Rotten / disgusting | "Rancid" |
| **A tip** | A dirty, messy place | "A dump" |
| **Fup** | Playful minced oath (from *Father Ted*) | "Fudge" |

The key difference from English is that the Irish tend to **avoid the noun** ("a pile of shite") and instead use **adjectives or character insults** — "that's a pure *manky* mess," "he's a feckin' *eejit*," "that's *gammy*." The swear word gets deflected into a milder descriptor, which keeps the social cost low.

So if you're writing a character who'd say "shite" in English, the Irish equivalent is more likely to be **"Ah, feck!"** or **"That's a right *manky* mess"** — same frustration, much lower heat.

## The Hierarchy

Here's a deeper look at how the Irish system actually works in practice, with more context on each word and how they combine.

One ranking from an Irish-language source puts the scale roughly like this:

| Tier | Words |
|---|---|
| **Strongest** | *fuck* (rarely said, but understood), *gobshite* |
| **Mid-tier** | *bollocks*, *eejit*, *arsehole*, *fecker* |
| **Mild / playful** | *shite*, *feck*, *dose*, *thick* |

So *shite* actually sits at the **milder end** of the Irish swear spectrum, not the middle. That's an important distinction from British English, where "shit" is mid-range.

## The Key Words in Detail

**Feck** — Not just "darn." It's a full grammatical word in Irish English. You can say *feck off*, *feck it*, *feckin' eejit*, *feck-all* (= nothing). It originated in Scots as an aphetic form of *effect* (meaning "value" or "worth"), and was repurposed as a minced oath for *fuck* from the 1940s onward. The adverbial *fecking* is attested in print from 1963. Its big cultural boost came from *Father Ted* on Channel 4, which needed a swear word that wouldn't breach broadcasting standards.

**Eejit** — A dialectal form of *idiot*, but the "eeeee" sound is stretched for comedic effect. On its own it's mild; add *feckin'* and it becomes the single most common Irish insult: *"You feckin' eeeeejit."* It's almost always said with affection.

**Gobshite** — *Gob* = mouth, *shite* = nonsense. A person who talks nonsense. It's the most "Irish" swear word and has crossed over into global English. It's stronger than *eejit* but still funnier than it is mean.

**Manky** — Gross, dirty, disgusting. The go-to adjective when something is physically unpleasant. *"That's a manky mess."*

**Gammy** — Broken, faulty, not working. *"My phone's gone gammy."* Very common in everyday speech, zero offensive content.

**Dryshite** — A party pooper, someone who kills the *craic*. Contains *shite* but is considered mild and humorous. *"Don't be a dryshite, come out dancing."*

**Dope** — Not the drug. In Irish English it means "slow" or "lazy," used in a nudge: *"Hurry the feck on, ya dope!"*

## The Unique Irish Construction

This is the part that has no real British equivalent. Irish speakers use a formulaic construction with *shite* (or other mild swears) to express **emphatic denial or contradiction**:

- **"I will in me hole"** = "I absolutely will not."
- **"She did and her shite"** = "She definitely did not."
- **"I am in me shite"** = "I am not."

The *shite* or *hole* or *bollocks* slot in is almost decorative — it's the *in/and* construction that does the work. You'll hear grandmothers say *"I will in me hole"* on a bus without batting an eye. This is the single most distinctly Irish swearing pattern.

## How They Combine in Real Speech

The Irish tendency is to **stack** mild words for emphasis rather than escalate to a stronger swear:

> *"Ah, that's a feckin' pile of manky shite, and the fecker who did it is a gobshite."*

Every word in that sentence is individually mild. Together they sound colourful and forceful, but none of them would get you a warning from a school principal. The escalation path is *feck → feckin' → feckin' + eejit/gobshite*, not *shite → shit → fuck*.

## For Writers

If you're writing an Irish character, the most natural "shite" substitute depends on context:

| Situation | British English | Irish equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Something's broken | "This is shit" | "This is **gammy**" / "This is a **manky** mess" |
| Someone's being an idiot | "You're an idiot" | "You're a feckin' **eejit**" |
| General frustration | "Shite!" | "**Ah feck!**" |
| Denying something | "I won't" | "**I will in me hole**" |
| Someone's talking nonsense | "You're full of shit" | "You're a **gobshite**" |
| Something's a terrible place | "This place is shit" | "This place is an awful **tip**" |

The guiding principle: Irish English **deflects** the noun (the thing that's bad) into an adjective or a character insult, keeping the social temperature low while sounding just as colourful.

## narrativised bibliography

No single book fills that exact slot — a prescriptive usage guide to Irish slang written with the same satirical, erudite, slightly drunk voice as *Usage & Abusage*. But there are a few things that get close from different angles:

**The serious reference (the "correct usage" part):**

- **A Dictionary of Hiberno-English** by Terence Patrick Dolan (Gill & Macmillan, 3rd ed.) — the definitive academic reference. Dry, thorough, no wit. It's the *Fowler's Modern English Usage* of Hiberno-English, which is to say it's the opposite of *Usage & Abusage* in tone. If you want to know whether *feckin'* or *feckin'* is "correct," this is the book.

- **Brewer's Dictionary of Irish Phrase and Fable** — closer to the O'Brien register. Witty entries, covers dialect and slang that never made it into standard dictionaries. Has the kind of marginalia you'd expect from a Myles na Gopál column.

**The literary voice (the "as if written by Flann O'Brien" part):**

- **Flann O'Brien's own Myles na Gopál columns** in *The Irish Times* (collected as At Last I Am Happy) — this is the closest thing. The columns are full of exactly the kind of "correct usage" asides, etymological digressions, and dismissive asides about English that you're looking for. *Usage & Abusage* is essentially one long Myles na Gopál column.

- **Martin McDonagh's plays** — The Cripple of Inishmaan has the famous "Oh thank Christ the fecker's over. A pile of fecking shite" line. Not a usage guide, but the language is deployed with the same precision and contempt.

**The single best modern article:**

- **Stan Carey's column** "What does 'feck' really mean?" in The Journal (2012) — traces the etymology, gives literary examples from Amanda Whittington, Peter Murphy, and Martin McDonagh, and lands somewhere between a usage guide and a Myles na Gopál column. It's the closest thing to a modern *Usage & Abusage* entry for a single word.

**The honest answer:** nobody has written a *Usage & Abusage* for Irish slang. The gap exists. Dolan's dictionary has the content but not the voice; O'Brien has the voice but only in fragments. If you're writing one, the brief is clear: take Dolan's entries, run them through Myles na Gopál's filter, and you've got the book.
