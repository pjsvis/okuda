# Silo genesis — container, regularisation, founding brief

> **The practice is not new.** Neither the container, nor the index, nor the
> founding statement, nor the citation. Archives did this, scriptoria did this,
> and the apparatus built to survive the print flood — the index, the footnote,
> the journal, the citation index — did this. What is new is only the *surplus*:
> a substrate that generates text faster than anyone generates records. This
> note names an old sequence precisely enough to run on that substrate.

## The sequence

**container → regularise → founding brief**

### 1. The container

A silo is a directory with a boundary. Everything for one subject or project
lives inside it, and the boundary does two jobs: it is the **unit of citation**
(a reference resolves *within* the silo, so a stranger can follow it) and the
**unit of context** (a fresh participant reads the container and knows the
project). Create it early, while there is almost nothing in it — a container
built after the fact is a prune.

### 2. Regularisation

Bring the initial material in and make it addressable **at birth**, while that
is cheap:

- an index for every directory that accumulates (`if it accumulates files, it
  gets an index`);
- a naming convention with the date in the name, so `ls` sorts and the date can
  be checked;
- frontmatter that declares the document's type and status;
- a callsign where something will cite it by name — not before;
- a gate that passes, once.

This is the moment the registry playbook was written about. **The five prune
batches are what it costs to skip this step** — retro-fitting an index to a pile
is editorial work, and it involves decisions (what is a refusal? what was built?)
that are much easier to make when there are four documents rather than four
hundred.

### 3. The founding brief

Write the founding brief. It states **aims *and* non-goals** — `docs/2026-08-20-origin-doc.md`
is the model, and its *Out of scope* section is the half most founding documents
omit. And its role is not documentation.

## The founding brief is the drift instrument

> **Drift is only visible against a written aim.**

Drift has two directions, and the second is quieter:

1. **failed to pursue** what the project set out to do;
2. **pursued what the project refused** — which is why the non-goals matter more
   than they look. A founding brief without them can only detect the first.

Without a founding brief, drift is **invisible**: the silo accumulates documents
that each look fine, and nothing is there to compare them against. With one, drift
becomes **checkable** — put the new brief beside the founding one and the
difference is a fact rather than a mood. That is *cite or mark* applied at the
level of purpose, and it makes the founding brief the silo's **audit surface for
intent**.

## Why non-silo-aware substrates drift

Not because the substrate is poor. Because it answers the **mean** — the default
shape of *"a brief about a project like this"* — since it cannot see *this*
project's aims. **Drift is the signature of a substrate without the context.**

It is the same law as **frame defaulting** (one answer) and **the default shape**
(one product), now at the level of a project. Call it **aim drift**: the frame a
substrate fails to bind is the project's own purpose — and the purpose is the one
frame that exists nowhere except in this container.

## The fix is a location, not an integration

Move the chat *inside* the silo. The substrate becomes a **participant** rather
than a vendor's session.

The corpus is the context: one copy, versioned, citable. Compare the market's
answer to the same problem — custom instructions, project knowledge, vendor
memory — which is a **per-vendor copy of your context**, and copies drift. This
is `no parallel stores` at the level of a project, and it is why the move stays
cheap: no connector, no plugin, no agent layer. The substrate is located where
the record is.

## The refinery rule

The honest counterweight: **a silo-aware substrate inherits the silo's own mean.**
Give a model the corpus and it will imitate it — align on the conventions, and
lose novelty. You get documents that fit beautifully and add nothing, which is a
different failure from drift and just as quiet.

So the rule is not *always inside*:

- **inside** for work that must **align** — a brief against the aims, a debrief
  against the acceptance criteria, a disposition against the keep-class;
- **outside** for work that must **surprise** — the wild first draft, the
  outside precedent, the thing a mean-constrained reader would not think of;
- **regularise whatever comes back in.**

The silo is a **refinery, not a fortress**: the boundary is where external
material is brought and made addressable, not a wall. The sequence says it
already — *move the initial material in, then regularise.*

## On the coinage — the honesty note

The terms this corpus keeps minting — **audit surface**, **attributable
structure**, **silo genesis** — are new *names*. The practices are centuries
old: the archive and its catalogue (Callimachus' *Pinakes*), the scriptorium, the
print flood's apparatus of address (index, footnote, journal, citation index),
and the republic of letters the whole workflow resembles.

Re-naming is not invention. It is **making an old discipline precise for a
substrate that has the surplus without the apparatus** — and the risk of a fresh
name is that it reads as a discovery. It is not. Every entry in `system.md`
already carries a **Prior art** line for exactly this reason; this section is the
same admission, made once, in public. **The prior art is the point** — if a term
here cannot cite its ancestors, it is a coinage looking for a mint, not a tool.
