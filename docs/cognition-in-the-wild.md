## The publisher's description

The MIT Press's own framing, quoted in full — it states the argument about as compactly as the book does:

> Edwin Hutchins combines his background as an anthropologist and an open ocean racing sailor and navigator in this account of how anthropological methods can be combined with cognitive theory to produce a new reading of cognitive science. 

> His theoretical insights are grounded in an extended analysis of ship navigation—its computational basis, its historical roots, its social organization, and the details of its implementation in actual practice aboard large ships. The result is an unusual interdisciplinary approach to cognition in culturally constituted activities outside the laboratory—"in the wild."

> Hutchins examines a set of phenomena that have fallen in the cracks between the established disciplines of psychology and anthropology, bringing to light a new set of relationships between culture and cognition. 

> The standard view is that culture affects the cognition of individuals. Hutchins argues instead that cultural activity systems have cognitive properties of their own that are different from the cognitive properties of the individuals who participate in them. 

> Each action for bringing a large naval vessel into port, for example, is informed by culture: the navigation team can be seen as a cognitive and computational system.

> Introducing Navy life and work on the bridge, Hutchins makes a clear distinction between the cognitive properties of an individual and the cognitive properties of a system. In striking contrast to the usual laboratory tasks of research in cognitive science, he applies the principal metaphor of cognitive science—cognition as computation (adopting David Marr's paradigm)—to the navigation task. 

> After comparing modern Western navigation with the method practiced in Micronesia, Hutchins explores the computational and cognitive properties of systems that are larger than an individual. He then turns to an analysis of learning or change in the organization of cognitive systems at several scales. 

> Hutchins's conclusion illustrates the costs of ignoring the cultural nature of cognition, pointing to the ways in which contemporary cognitive science can be transformed by new meanings and interpretations.

— The MIT Press

## What the book is

Fieldwork begun in 1980 — Hutchins aboard a US Navy ship entering Seattle through the Straits of Juan de Fuca and Puget Sound — and a formal study from the summer of 1984, while he was a Personnel Research Psychologist at the Navy Personnel Research and Development Center in San Diego. The site is the navigation bridge of a US Navy ship. Personnel and vessel are pseudonymous; the reported discourse is transcribed from audio recordings of actual events, which is where the book's evidentiary weight sits.

He came to it as a racing-yacht navigator, and records the moment the frame shifted: *"It was only after I completed my first study period at sea that I realized the importance of the fact that cognition was socially distributed."*

## The move

The unit of analysis leaves the skull:

> "I will move the boundaries of the cognitive unit of analysis out beyond the skin of the individual person and treat the navigation team as a cognitive and computational system."

The boundaries being dissolved were, in his account, "erected, primarily for analytic convenience, in social space, in physical space, and in time." The aim is stated flatly: *"This book is an attempt to put cognition back into the social and cultural world."*

## What "in the wild" means

Defined, and defined against two neighbours it is not:

> "The phrase 'cognition in the wild' refers to human cognition in its natural habitat — that is, to naturally occurring culturally constituted human activity."

Not Lévi-Strauss's *pensée sauvage*; not the inverse of Goody's domesticated mind. The axis is the laboratory against the everyday world — cognition "in captivity" versus cognition adapting "to its natural surroundings." The metaphor is meant to evoke "a sense of an ecology of thinking in which human cognition interacts with an environment rich in organizing resources."

One methodological claim follows, and matters more now than it did in 1995: "It is notoriously difficult to generalize laboratory findings to real-world situations." Real-world study is not thereby applied work — "There are many excellent reasons to look at the 'real world' that are not concerned with hoped-for applications... Pure research on the nature of real cognitive practices is needed." And practice is where culture lives: "it is in real practice that culture is produced and reproduced... In practice we see the connection between history and the future and between cultural structure and social structure."

## The thesis

Cognition is not merely coloured by culture; it is constituted by it:

> "human cognition is not just influenced by culture and society, but that it is in a very fundamental sense a cultural and social process."

The antagonist is cognitive anthropology's ideational turn, which lodged knowledge "inside the individual" and reduced cognition to declarative knowledge — what people can say about what they know. *"Skill went out the window of the 'white room.'"* The book answers Geertz's 1983 call for an "outdoor psychology," which cognitive anthropology "was unable or unwilling to be," and argues that the cognitive revolution's narrowing assumptions "have become a burden, and they prevent us from seeing the nature of human cognition."

## The book, chapter by chapter

### 1. Welcome Aboard

Three journeys into the field. **Physical space:** street → base → ship → bridge, the nesting of the work environment that both constrains and supports the activity. **Social space:** civilian to accepted observer, through the officer/enlisted divide, branch identities, and rank — treated as variables *in* the cognitive system, not as backdrop. **Conceptual space:** from everyday wayfinding into a professional technical domain with its own vocabulary, tools, and founding question, *"Where am I?"* The chapter opens on a casualty — loss of steam pressure — and shows the bridge absorbing it as a whole, an early demonstration that no individual holds the task.

### 2. Navigation as Computation

Marr's three levels applied to navigation: computational theory (what and why), representation and algorithm (how), implementation (in what substrate). At the computational level all navigation is one problem — fix a two-dimensional position by combining one-dimensional position constraints. **Lines of position** (visual bearings, radio directions) and **circles of position** (radar range, celestial observation), plus **position-displacement constraints** (dead reckoning) and **distance–rate–time constraints**. That level is culture-independent. The divergence appears a level down. Western navigation runs on a single abstract global grid — latitude and longitude, Mercator charts, standardised units — with computation done by geometric construction and numerical work on the chart; Hutchins calls the chart a **"simulation computer."** Micronesian navigation runs on an egocentric, dynamic frame: the **sidereal compass** and the ***etak*** system, which tracks a voyage's progress by imagining a reference island moving against a fixed star field, dividing the passage into stages. A system held in the head, and equally precise. A historical review then shows the implementation level to be contingent — astrolabe to sextant, chip log to modern instruments. Modern practice and its tools are "contingent on complex historical processes," and "the accumulation of structure in the tools of the trade is itself a cognitive process."

### 3. The Implementation of Contemporary Pilotage

The book's central definition: cognition is **"a conception of computation as the propagation of representational state across a variety of media."** Traced through the **fix cycle**, one bearing passes through six representations: the real spatial relation of ship to landmark → the physical angle on the **pelorus** (analog) → the reading on the compass dial (analog→digital) → the spoken number and the recorder's log (digital) → the angle set on the **Hoey protractor** (digital→analog) → a line of position drawn on the chart (geometric). Cognition is in none of those places; it is the relay between them. Hence the **functional system**: a person alone cannot draw a line of position, but person + pelorus + Hoey + chart can, and the composite has properties the person does not. Tools restructure the task rather than merely assisting it — they internalise constraints until reasoning becomes perception and manipulation. Computation is also distributed in **time** through **pre-computation**: planned courses, turn points, and danger zones are plotted before the ship sails, converting a future computation into a reading task.

The worked example is the **three-minute rule**, in Hutchins' own later account of it:

> "A nautical mile is very nearly 2000 yards, and an hour is exactly 60 minutes. This means that three minutes is one-twentieth of an hour and 100 yards is one twentieth part of a nautical mile. Thus, the number of hundreds of yards traveled by an object in three minutes equals the speed of the object in nautical miles per hour."

Two successive fixes three minutes apart, 1500 yards between them on the chart: the span is taken with the dividers, transferred to the yard scale, and read as **15 knots** — the division never performed, only the label read with two zeros ignored (the book, pp. 151–152).

Two further claims carry the chapter. First, the functional system — "local functional systems composed of a person in interaction with a tool have cognitive properties that are radically different from the cognitive properties of the person alone." Second, the line that does the most work for anyone building instruments: "The environments of human thinking are not 'natural' environments. They are artificial through and through. Humans create their cognitive powers by creating the environments in which they exercise those powers."

### 4. The Organization of Team Performances

The team is the computational unit and its social organisation is the architecture. The **Sea and Anchor Detail** — plotter, recorder, bearing takers — is a network of processing nodes, and the chapter shows three things the organisation buys. **Parallelism:** two bearing takers watch different landmarks at once. **Buffering:** the recorder is a human buffer, absorbing bearings that arrive out of order and feeding the plotter at a pace he can handle — the fix for a mismatch in processing speeds between parts of the system. **Redundancy:** the bridge fix is checked against the radar fix from Combat Information Center, which is how errors surface at all.

Then the book's sharpest coordination case. The rule *take the bearing nearest the beam first, because it changes fastest* is trivial for one person and hard for two: neither taker can see the other's workload or the whole picture, so the locally obvious rule produces waiting — the busier taker holds, the less busy one is idle. The optimum decouples **observation** from **reporting** and lets the taker with more work report first. The participants struggle to find it, because no one can step outside his own field of view. That gap is the cognitive seam, and the chapter's real finding: a strategy that is simple for an individual does not simply scale to the team.

The general claim, and the one the book is remembered for: "systems of socially distributed cognition may have interesting cognitive properties of their own"; and "Depending on their organization, groups must have cognitive properties that are not predictable from a knowledge of the properties of the individuals in the group." Note what carries the weight — *depending on their organization*. The team is "a flexible connective tissue that maintains the propagation of representational state in the face of a range of potentially disruptive events," and the work-organization problem is real: "two or more heads are [not] always better than one."

### 5. Communication

Communication patterns, not just content, generate group cognitive properties. The chapter models a "community of networks": each individual a constraint-satisfaction network that exhibits **confirmation bias** — once an interpretation forms, contrary evidence does not dislodge it — with communication as the transfer of activation between networks. Three regimes. Heavy early communication fuses the group into one large network: consensus arrives fast, on the strength of initial dispositions rather than of the evidence, and is then rigid. No communication leaves the group unable to converge at all. The effective pattern is **explore separately first, then combine** — preserving interpretive diversity until the evidence has been weighed. Hence the chapter's conclusion, and the book's most counterintuitive claim: **more communication is not always better.** It is a mechanism-level account of groupthink, and it locates the fix in the *timing and structure* of communication rather than in its bandwidth — the pattern juries and brainstorming are built on.

### 6. The Context of Learning

The workplace is a designed learning environment. A quartermaster's career runs **depth sounder operator → bearing taker → bearing recorder → plotter** — the same order as the information flow of the fix cycle. The isomorphism produces an overlapping distribution of knowledge: the expert has worked every junior job, the novice only his own. Two consequences follow. Error diagnosis is cheap — a plotter can catch a misread digit and know precisely why it was misread, having made the same mistake. And teaching comes pre-calibrated, because the expert has stood in the position he is correcting. The chapter's second concept is the **horizon of observation**: what a learner can see, hear, and reach determines what he can learn. Open interaction and open tools — the chart on the table, visible to everyone — enlarge it; private ones, such as a calculator screen only one person can see, shrink it.

### 7. Learning in Context

Learning is not the transfer of information inward; it is **adaptive reorganisation** of a system that includes the person *and* the material setting. Taking "learning a written procedure" apart: the novice leans on the external medium — the procedure sheet — using meta-intermediary structures such as a top-to-bottom reading strategy. With practice, organisation forms in **internal media**: *lexical* (the word order of the steps, recitable), *semantic* (what the steps mean and how they relate), *motor* (an automatised action sequence that runs without deliberation). Learning is a wave of organisation propagating from the sheet into the body and back out as skilled action. This also accounts mechanically for tacit knowledge: once the motor medium dominates, the lexical and semantic scaffolding that was used to learn the task is no longer activated, which is why the expert asked *how he does it* cannot say.

### 8. Organizational Learning

The book's set piece, and Hutchins was on the bridge video-recording when it happened. Entering a narrow channel, a large ship lost its main gyrocompass. The crew could no longer read a true bearing off the instrument; every bearing now had to be computed — true bearing = compass heading + deviation + magnetic variation + relative bearing, or **TB = C + D + V + RB**. They worked through computational variations of **TB = C + V + RB** across **thirty-eight lines of position** before discovering that a term was missing: **deviation**. With it restored, the ability to plot accurate positions returned.

The discovery arrived as an "Aha!" that the computation alone does not predict. What drove it was the growing size of the fix triangles — the visible degradation of the fixes is what sent the plotter hunting for an explanation. The organisational point is the chapter's title: no one designed and issued the workable routine. It emerged from local adjustments under pressure, each person responding to the problem immediately in front of him — **evolution rather than design**, "the kinds of processes that must be the engines of cultural change."

### 9. Cultural Cognition

The synthesis, and the book's most consequential argument. Cognitive science treats culture as content or context lying outside cognition; Hutchins argues culture is constitutive of it. He retells the history from the other end: the Turing machine's prototype was never an isolated brain but **a person computing with paper and pencil** — person, symbols, and rules together in one system. The pioneers' achievement was to abstract that computation *out* of the person–tool system and automate it. The error came next, when the abstraction was put back **into** the head and called the architecture of the mind. With the actor removed, the hand, the eye, and the ear fell off the model — which is where cognitive science's disembodied predicament comes from. Hence the formulation the book is remembered for: the physical symbol system is a model of a sociocultural system *from which the human actor has been removed*. The book closes by placing cognition in culture on **three nested timescales**: the conduct of the activity (minutes to hours), the development of the practitioners (days to years), and the evolution of the practice (decades to millennia).

## The vocabulary

The book's terms, for when it is not to hand.

| Term | Means |
|---|---|
| Fix / fix cycle | Determining the ship's position; the recurring routine, and the book's unit of micro-analysis |
| Line / circle of position | A one-dimensional position constraint (a bearing, a radar range); two or more fix a 2-D position |
| Dead reckoning | Estimating the next position from present position, heading, and planned speed |
| Propagation of representational state | The book's definition of cognition: state travelling and transforming across media |
| Functional system | Person + tool, with cognitive properties the person alone does not have |
| Pre-computation | Doing work before it is needed, so the live task becomes reading rather than reasoning |
| Computational architecture | Social organisation read as the structure that runs the computation |
| Activity score | Hutchins' timeline notation for showing a team's parallel activities and interactions |
| Horizon of observation | What a learner can see, hear, and reach — the ceiling on what he can learn |
| Internal media | Lexical, semantic, motor: the forms organisation takes as skill is acquired |
| Three timescales | Activity conduct / practitioner development / practice evolution |

## Praise (the reception, on the record)

The publisher's page carries two endorsements:

> Hutchins argument that 'the physical symbol system is a model of the operation of the sociocultural system from which the human actor has been removed' has great force. Hutchins challenge to the formal account of human cognition is based on what he knows about how humans actually navigate their way through the world, not on a philosophic doctrine. Overall, I think this is a great book. It has lots of ideas, rich data, and innovative analyses. It is highly disciplined, clearly written, and thoughtful. I believe it will be a real landmark for other naviagators of the cognitive ocean.
> — Roy G. D'Andrade, Department of Anthropology, University of California

> This timely work has the potential to make important contributions to cognitive science, artificial intelligence and computer supported cooperative work communities. It addresses difficult problems of great importance with novelty and creativity. The general area of situated cognition is a lively and important one, spanning areas as diverse as anthropology, robotics, and office information systems. There are two fundamental persepctives that are central to these efforts: that cognition must be studied in realistic contexts, and when viewed in these contexts much of what we consider intelligence is really not in the head but out in the world. […] Hutchins tackles both of these questions in new and intriguing ways.
> — James Martin, Computer Science Department, University of Colorado

Quoted as the publisher prints them, typos intact (*naviagators*, *persepctives*); the bracketed ellipsis marks a cut. One thing worth knowing when weighing the first: **D'Andrade was Hutchins' own doctoral supervisor** in cognitive anthropology — a teacher's endorsement, not a stranger's.

## Hume's Razor, applied to the book

One ship, one team, mid-1980s US Navy, pseudonymous, observed by a single ethnographer — generalised into a theory of cognition as such. The transcribed discourse is genuinely strong evidence; the sample is one. "Groups have cognitive properties not predictable from individuals" is a claim about *organisation*, demonstrated rather than experimentally isolated. **Convergent with the lineage it sits in, not a proof of it.** The book is best read the way the silo reads it: as the load-bearing account of why an externalised representation does the cognitive work, offered by someone who watched it happen on a bridge.

The sharpest published objection is Cosma Shalizi's, writing in 1998: that Hutchins never shows collective computation to be anything other than a special environment for ordinary symbol-processing problem-solvers, and that no actual theory of collective cognition is supplied — "Hutchins doesn't so much theorize as wave his hands with vigor and emphasis." On Shalizi's reading the chart is an external representation *for the quartermaster*, not an internal one for the ship, and nothing in the book demands a representation that cannot be localised to a person. It is a serious objection and it does not require the book to be wrong; it requires the book to be read for its evidence rather than its metaphysics. Worth holding in view.

- **The fix cycle is a discovery contract.** Bearings are taken, reduced to lines of position, resolved into one fix — a compact answer assembled from redundant sources, each capable of being wrong, with the instrument honest about its own bounds. The dive is the same shape, one scale down.

## The one line

Cognition is not what happens inside a head but what propagates between heads and instruments; the environment of thought is artificial and we build it deliberately; and the group's properties are its organisation's, not its members'.
