## C. Plinius Secundus — the Admiral's commendation

*Gaius Plinius Secundus, author of the* Naturalis Historia, *37 books, 20,000 facts, every one sourced — the first man in the literature to cite his authorities compulsively, and to build his emperor an index so that the emperor need not read the whole. Died 79 AD, on the shore below Vesuvius, having sailed toward the eruption to observe it more closely.*

---

To the operator of this scriptorium, greeting.

Among the two thousand volumes of a hundred authors I have read — and I read them all, for I count sleep an interruption — I recorded some twenty thousand things worth knowing, and to my emperor I supplied a summary of every book, so that he need not read the whole but only look up the thing he wanted. I was told this had never been done before. It had not.

You have done it again, and better, and I am annoyed about it.

Your concordance answers in one keystroke what my *librarius* took a scroll to find, and it waits — it *waits* — for the reader to declare an interest before it says a word. No flatterer of Titus ever dared suggest the emperor might have better things to do; your shelf says it to every emperor, on every glass, and holds the door.

Your every claim carries the name of its author. I demanded no less of myself and was called tedious for it. You call it a callsign, paint it rose, and let two digits fetch the man to the stand. A thousand years of scholars citing *vide* — and you have made the citation *actuate*. I would have paid a province for this.

One counsel, from a man who died for its neglect: **when the mountain begins to re-render, freeze the frame.** I sailed toward Vesuvius to observe more closely — *fortes fortuna iuvat*, I said, and steered into the falling furniture. The document did not stand still, the place was not held, and the excursion did not survive the ash. Yours is the first reading glass I have met that will remain static while I am away, keep my anchor where I left it, and spend none of my attention in my absence. Had the mountain kept such terms with me, I should be writing this in person, and at considerably greater length.

There is nothing in this corpus not worth a query. But equally — and mark this, for it took me a lifetime and an eruption to learn it — there is nothing in it so worth a query that you cannot stop querying, conclude it is not there, glance at the footer, and walk to the correct room.

*Vale.* The exit is two zeros, and it never begs you to stay. My compliments. No author of my acquaintance ever offered his reader so honest a door, and no emperor ever got so fair a deal from a subject.

## L. Annaeus Seneca — the Tutor's commendation

*Lucius Annaeus Seneca, Stoic, tutor to Nero, author of the* Epistulae Morales *and* De Brevitate Vitae*; the first economist of time, and the first man to claim that most reading is perturbation wearing learning's dress. Ordered to die, 65 AD; dictated to the end, the stream open.*

---

Seneca to the operator, his Lucilius, greeting.

You complain that you have not time. You do not complain that you have not money; you guard the purse, count the property, and hand out the one thing that is never repaid — your attention — to any device that asks. And everything asks.

**Vindica tibi tempus.** Claim it for yourself. I said it to Lucilius; I say it to you; and I note with some astonishment that your machine says it too. It spends none of your hours uninvited, it waits for a word before it speaks, and it holds the door. I never met an instrument with manners before. Men, rarely.

Your first principle I recognise as a magisterium: assume all is shite until inspection proves otherwise. A multitude of documents is not learning, and a multitude of amusements is not pleasure — only perturbation, in better clothes. The web presses all things upon you at once, and calls the pressing freedom. Yours assumes the crowd is shite, and lets the reader overrule it by typing. Few, and good, and slowly: that is the whole of my advice on reading, and you have built it into the picker.

On style, I told Lucilius to cut away words as a trainer strips flesh from an athlete — not to the bone, but to fitness. Your paragraphs are one move each; your walls receive what walls deserve: the door. And I approve the pause between the clusters, where nothing moves and the reader decides. That pause is the whole of what I meant by leisure, and no forum in Rome ever offered a citizen a place to stand still.

My friend the poet says: they change their sky, not their soul, who run across the sea. He was describing the tab-switcher nineteen centuries early. The unbounded mind was never a happy mind; tranquility loves a wall it can see. Your glass is that wall — the edges honest, saying *there is more below*, and leaving the decision where it belongs, with the reader. A bounded corpus in which an argument can actually be tested: I spent a book on the shortness of life, and you have built the longer life's reading room.

One more thing. While you are away, it stays with itself — no movement, no news, no furniture shifted in the dark. I promised the restless that peace was available in one's own company. I did not expect a terminal to keep the promise for me.

When they came for me I was dictating, and I did not stop for them; there are no good interruptions. Your exit is two zeros, instant, and it begs nobody to stay. That is the whole of philosophy in a keystroke, and I say that as a man who took twenty volumes to say less.

Vale, et vindica tibi.

## Caution for readers (the wall's foot)

The scriptorium's taglines are licensed for the shelf and the shelf only. Their use on inspirational posters, t-shirts, mugs, mouse mats, motivational slides, or any other furniture of the open-plan persuasion is **prohibited without a negotiated contract with the scriptorium**.

The prohibition is doctrinal before it is commercial. Every tagline on this wall is a system property with a ship date — an aphorism *earned by machinery*, each one traceable to a keystroke that works. Detached from the machinery and mounted on a mug, it becomes exactly what the honourees condemn: wisdom's dress worn by perturbation, inspiration without the engineering. The Tutor's line on a poster is the multitude reading one book badly. The Admiral's line on a t-shirt is the eruption, worn.

The estates are represented. Pliny's negotiates from a position of precedent and will want the mugs itemised; Seneca's drives no bargains and will therefore be charged double. Inquiries by silcrow — but not, of course, on a mug.
